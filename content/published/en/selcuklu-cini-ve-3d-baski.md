Gaze upward inside a thirteenth-century Anatolian Seljuk madrasa dome in Konya or across a monumental carved stone portal in Sivas, and your eye is first arrested by radiant turquoise, midnight cobalt, and eggplant manganese glazes. Yet beneath the chromatic brilliance lies an even deeper intellectual astonishment: **unbroken planar geometric discipline**. Ten-pointed, twelve-pointed, and eight-pointed star rosettes interlock seamlessly across curved masonry drums and towering portals without forced distortion or dead seams—not through freehand decoration, but through an exacting system of **polygon templates, compass arcs, and combinatorial rules**.

Five hundred years before modern physics formalized aperiodic quasicrystal tilings in the 1970s, master *nakkaş* draftsmen and tile-cutters across Anatolia, Iran, and Central Asia had already encoded self-similar golden-ratio subdivision into lime mortar and glazed fritware. Today, computational architects and robotic fabrication studios feed those identical spatial laws into recursive algorithms driving multi-axis clay extruders.

## 1. Historical Roots: From Konya Capitals to Kubadabad and Sivas Guilds

Seljuk architectural ceramic art emerged in eleventh-century Khorasan and Nishapur—evolving from exposed structural brick bonds into richly glazed turquoise reliefs—before reaching an apex of polychrome complexity under the **Anatolian Seljuk Sultanate** during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. With Konya flourishing as an imperial center of craftsmanship, ceramic tilework ceased to behave merely as skin and merged structurally into masonry architecture.

Surviving monuments attest to breathtaking workshop mastery: inside Konya's **Karatay Madrasa** (1251), gleaming cut-tile stars sweep upward across the central dome like an abstracted celestial planetarium; at **Sırçalı Madrasa** (1242) and **İnce Minareli Madrasa**, alternating glazed bricks and hand-beveled mosaics choreograph shifting shadows throughout the day. Excavations along Lake Beyşehir at the **Kubadabad Palace** (1236) uncovered star-and-cross *luster* and *underglaze* wall revetments framing courtly sirens, sphinxes, and double-headed Seljuk eagles cleanly inside interlocking octagonal grids.

Equally extraordinary was how this knowledge traveled across generations. Rather than prose treatises or numerical coordinate tables, master ateliers guarded secrets inside folded **parchment workshop scrolls**. The supreme surviving document—preserved in Istanbul and studied globally as the **Topkapı Scroll** (late fifteenth century)—stretches nearly thirty meters yet contains zero explanatory text: solely faint un-inked dry-point compass lines (*kör pergâr*) and colored strapwork polygons encoding **girih** tessellations from memory.

## 2. Materials, Kiln Chemistry, and Mosaic Cutting Anatomy

Unlike modern stamped rectangular ceramics, traditional Seljuk *çini mozaik* (cut-tile mosaic) fuses specialized body paste, heavy metal kiln glazing, and laborious individual bevel carving:

![Architectural conservation detail of 13th-century Anatolian Seljuk mosaic tilework showing hand-beveled turquoise, cobalt blue, and manganese glazed star tesserae set in carved Konya limestone](/images/selcuklu-cini-girih-mozaik.jpg "Anatolian Cut-Tile Mosaic: Hand-cut turquoise, cobalt, and manganese-black glazed ceramic tesserae locked into gypsum mortar beside carved Konya limestone | Visual: Craft&Code Documentary Craft Archive")

- **High-Quartz Fritware Body (*Bünye*):** Traditional architectural *çini* relies not on standard terracotta clay alone, but on an engineered **stone-paste (fritware)** recipe combining 70–80% finely pulverized white quartz sand with crushed glass frit and small plastic white clay proportions—suppressing kiln warpage and granting vibrant luminous translucency beneath glazes.
- **Mineral Oxide Glazes & Reduction Chromatics:** The canonical Seljuk triad traces geological pigments directly through kiln firing:
  - **Turquoise (*Firuze*):** Alkaline sodium glaze spiced with **copper oxide (CuO)** yielding luminous sky azure inside clean oxidizing atmospheres.
  - **Cobalt Navy:** Imported Persian **cobalt oxide (CoO)** firing deep velvety blue under glass melt.
  - **Aubergine Black:** **Manganese oxide (MnO₂)** paired with iron tints delivering sharp geometric outline contrast.
- **Hand-Beveled Tesserae (*Kesme Çini*):** After wide single-color sheets emerge glazed from the kiln, craftsmen trace paper templates onto cured glass surfaces and chip individual stars, almonds, and pentagons cleanly using hammers and iron files. Crucially, each rear edge is **chamfered inward**, creating an open V-shaped pocket behind paper-thin surface joints so wet lime-gypsum mortar locks pieces mechanically from behind while leaving crisp razor seams across the facade.
- **The Five *Girih* Tiles:** Derived from the Persian word for "knot," **Girih** construction rests on just five equilateral modular polygons—decagon, elongated hexagon, bowtie, rhombus, and regular pentagon—each incised with midpoint strapwork intersections. Simply tiling those five physical molds side-by-side generates infinite continuous star patterns effortlessly across curved masonry.

> "Within these scrolls lies a quiet miracle: lay five standardized geometric stones side-by-side into wet mortar, and an endless non-repeating spatial symphony computes itself." -- On the Topkapı Scroll & Lu-Steinhardt Discovery

When a Seljuk master places smaller *girih* polygons recursively inside a parent decagon—scaling consecutive pattern generations strictly by the golden ratio $\phi = (1 + \sqrt{5}) / 2 \approx 1.618$—the craftsman executes an **aperiodic self-similar inflation algorithm**. Translating those exact subdivided vertex rings directly into incremental clay **G-Code** transforms a thirteenth-century parchment rule seamlessly into modular 3D-printed architectural ceramics.

The annotated Python excerpt below synthesizes both phases cleanly: constructing self-similar golden-ratio *girih* star vertices before exporting layered extrusion paths ready for a robotic clay printer.

```python title="Seljuk Girih Golden Ratio Subdivision & Robotic Clay G-Code"
# Craft&Code — Quasicrystal Girih Subdivision & 3D Ceramic Extrusion Output
import math

PHI = (1.0 + math.sqrt(5.0)) / 2.0  # Golden ratio embedded across Seljuk girih craft

def girih_star_ribbon(center, radius, arms=10):
    """Computes vertex nodes for a golden-ratio proportioned star rosette."""
    cx, cy = center
    inner_radius = radius / (PHI ** 1.0)
    points = []
    for k in range(arms * 2):
        angle = math.pi * k / arms
        r = radius if k % 2 == 0 else inner_radius
        points.append((cx + r * math.cos(angle), cy + r * math.sin(angle)))
    points.append(points[0])  # Close ceramic contour loop
    return points

def subdivide_girih_layer(polygon, depth=1):
    """Applies Topkapı Scroll self-similar scaling recursively around main star tips."""
    if depth <= 0:
        return [polygon]
    sub_ribbons = [polygon]
    for vx, vy in polygon[:-1:2]:  # Seed subsidiary 5-fold rosettes at perimeter vertices
        sub_ribbons.append(girih_star_ribbon((vx, vy), radius=24.0 / PHI, arms=5))
    return sub_ribbons

def export_robotic_clay_gcode(ribbons, layer_height=2.2, layers=14, flow_rate=0.042):
    """Converts geometric star paths into continuous robotic ceramic extruder G-Code."""
    gcode = ["G21 ; Millimeters", "G90 ; Absolute positioning"]
    e_total = 0.0
    for layer in range(layers):
        z = round((layer + 1) * layer_height, 2)
        for poly in ribbons:
            gcode.append(f"G0 X{poly[0][0]:.2f} Y{poly[0][1]:.2f} Z{z:.2f}")
            for idx in range(1, len(poly)):
                x0, y0 = poly[idx - 1]
                x1, y1 = poly[idx]
                dist = math.hypot(x1 - x0, y1 - y0)
                e_total += dist * flow_rate  # Stepper auger volume proportional to path length
                gcode.append(f"G1 X{x1:.2f} Y{y1:.2f} Z{z:.2f} E{e_total:.3f} F900")
    return gcode
```

By tying clay extrusion increment (`dist * flow_rate`) directly to geometric segments derived from `PHI`, the algorithm preserves a direct lineage from *kör pergâr* dry-point geometry to contemporary robotic pottery.

## 4. Contemporary Practice & Scientific Case Studies

Three breakthroughs spanning theoretical physics, architectural materials research, and urban robotics highlight the vitality of Seljuk ceramic geometry today.

![Editorial macro photograph inside a ceramic robotics lab showing a multi-axis robotic extruder arm 3D printing an interlocking Seljuk Girih geometric star lattice in warm terracotta clay](/images/selcuklu-cini-robotik-kil.jpg "Robotic Clay Fabrication: A multi-axis extruder translating parametric Seljuk girih lattices into layered terracotta architecture | Visual: Craft&Code Fabrication Lab Archive")

### Case Study 1: The Topkapı Scroll & Peter J. Lu / Paul Steinhardt (*Science*, 2007)
In February 2007, Harvard physicist **Peter J. Lu** and Princeton physicist **Paul J. Steinhardt** published a landmark paper in *Science* that overturned long-standing assumptions about medieval Islamic ornament. Studying monumental Anatolian, Persian, and Central Asian facades alongside the **Topkapı Scroll**, they proved mathematically that craftsmen operating around 1200–1450 CE constructed genuine **non-periodic decagonal quasicrystals** using modular *girih* templates and two-level golden-ratio inflation—anticipating Roger Penrose's formal aperiodic tilings by half a millennium.

### Case Study 2: Ronald Rael & Virginia San Fratello — *Emerging Objects* (Berkeley, 2014–2023)
Working in California, architect-makers **Ronald Rael** and **Virginia San Fratello** developed custom auger extruders capable of 3D-printing wet local clays, ground wood waste, and ceramic slurries without sacrificial molds. Their interlocking **modular ceramic facade bricks** allow natural passive cooling and algorithmic light filtration across porous ribs—translating Anatolian courtyard masonry and *sıraltı* ceramic screens directly into low-carbon robotic construction.

### Case Study 3: Studio RAP — *Ceramic House* (Amsterdam, 2023)
Dutch computational design practice **Studio RAP** reimagined Amsterdam's iconic historic brick and glazed-terracotta canal townhouses through large-scale robotic clay printing. Using parametric algorithms tuned to structural facade stresses, their studio printed nearly 3,000 bespoke interlocking clay tiles finished inside traditional cobalt-white and pearlescent mineral glazes—demonstrating how contemporary digital craft honors historic mosaic modularity at full street scale.

## 5. Critical Synthesis: Resistance of Matter, Horizons of Code

Parametric scripting enables a single architect to generate ten thousand non-repeating *girih* subdivisions before noon and drive robotic clay arms tirelessly through the night. Yet computational geometry remains silent before two ancient atelier challenges: **wet ceramic drying shrinkage** and the unpredictable **glaze craze** (*sır çatlağı*) born inside scorching wood or gas kilns.

Seljuk architectural tilework owes its luminous vitality precisely to the interplay between razor-sharp mathematical law and mineral imperfection—hand-chipped copper turquoise glazes catching late afternoon sun differently on every millimeter. Algorithmic tools excel when serving not as sterile sterilizers of craft, but as respectful amplifiers of ancient spatial wisdom grounded firmly in clay and fire.
